“Intention to Commit Corporate Murder”

Just before the war in Iran, the Department of Defense declared Anthropic, the company that makes the Claude AI chatbot, a “supply-chain risk” over a contract dispute. The contract dispute was over a contract that the DOD had already agreed to(!!) and Anthropic simply refused to modify the terms. Obviously it would be well within reason for the department to choose to not renew the contract if they did not like the terms, but they took it a step further, trying to force Anthropic to accept the modifications by threatening the supply-chain risk designation. This designation has never been applied to a domestic company; it is typically reserved for companies in foreign adversary countries. The designation would mean that Anthropic could not do any business with another federal contractor, which would include every other large technology company and some of Anthropic’s largest investors. Anthropic already won a preliminary injunction in court, but the case will go continue on.

Dean Ball served as an advisor for the Trump administration’s AI Action Plan, released last summer. He accurately described the Trump administration’s action as an “intention to commit corporate murder.” (Source) In a Tweet, he went on to describe the action as a “psychotic power grab” and a claim that “the DoD can force all contractors to stop doing business of any kind with arbitrary other companies.” (Source)

The Trump administration has taken other significant actions to grab power in private companies in a way that is unprecedented. However, this is a step even further, with the administration demanding that a private company do business with the government, or else it will effectively be shut down. It’s laughable for an administration theoretically representing the party of small government, and a party that would relentlessly criticize such actions from a Democratic administration, or a disfavored foreign nation.

Corruption

The Trump administration continues to provide financial benefits for political allies and Trump family members.

The Cato Institute, which is not a leftist organization, has analyzed what it describes as a “blizzard” of second-term pardons, noting that over $1.5 billion in criminal debt has been forgiven for political allies and donors. During the Biden administration, his pardons forgave a bit under $700,000. $700,000 may also sound like a lot, but it is 0.05% of the amount that has already been forgiven in one year of the Trump administration. (Source)

With actions in the war in Iran, there have been several instances of actions that may indicate insider trading by those with knowledge of Trump administration actions. At the beginning of the war, a Polymarket trader placed several suspiciously well-timed bets on actions related to the attacks on Iran. These bets made the trader over $500,000. Notably, the Trump family has significant ties to Polymarket, and the administration has dropped two investigations into Polymarket. (Source) A few weeks later, there was a very large amount of movement, over half a billion dollars in contracts, in the oil futures market just about 10 minutes before President Trump announced that the United States and Iran were in talks for “COMPLETE AND TOTAL RESOLUTION OF OUR HOSTILITIES IN THE MIDDLE EAST.” That announcement has obviously not yet borne out, but it appears that someone had significant financial benefits from the announcement. The Trump administration’s Commodity Futures Trading Commission does not yet appear to be interested in investigating these incidents. (Source)

Jared Kushner, Donald Trump’s son-in-law, serves as a negotiator in the MIddle East for the Trump administration. At the same time, he is raising money for his private equity firm from the very same governments with which he is negotiating. He has reportedly seeked to raise over $5 billion from these governments or groups closely tied with them. Since the administration has not made him an official government employee, he is not required to report conflicts of interest. (Source)

Courts vs. The Administration

The relationship between the federal judiciary and the Trump administration has continued to deteriorate. Judges have expressed serious frustration with the Department of Justice and Department of Homeland Security for violating court orders and appearing unprepared for cases. Judges appointed by presidents of both parties, including Trump-appointed judges, have ruled against the administration’s mass detention policy with an overwhelming majority. (Source)

In Minnesota, Chief Judge Patrick Schiltz warned of criminal contempt charges after finding the government had violated more than 200 court orders in his district alone — a level of defiance he called historic. (Source)

Nationally, there have been at least 35 instances since August where judges have demanded that the administration explain why it should not be punished for violating court orders. (Source)

A judge in New Jersey ruled that federal prosecutors have lost their credibility by “intentionally” ignoring judicial mandates. (Source)

The Justice Department itself acknowledged violating more than 50 court orders in New Jersey, offering “sky-high caseloads” as the explanation for illegal deportations and missed deadlines. (Source)

ICE Enforcement: Who Is Actually Being Targeted?

The administration has consistently claimed it is focused on dangerous criminals. The data tells a different story. And cruelty continues in the treatment of those detainees who have no criminal record, from releasing detainees in the cold without their personal items or any way to contact anyone for assistance, to failures to provide medical care inside detention centers and reported betting pools about suicidal detainees.

Nearly 75% of ICE detainees have no criminal conviction, according to ICE’s own data. (Source)

One Trump-appointed judge harshly criticized Department of Homeland Security treatment of a man with no criminal record, who entered the U.S. at age 9. He described the treatment as “unimaginable cruelty” and said, “the laws of human decency condemn such villainy.” (Source)

A different Trump-appointed judge found that ICE and CBP have adopted policies authorizing investigatory stops based solely on race or ethnicity — not on any individualized suspicion of wrongdoing. (Source)

A Wall Street Journal investigation found that the majority of people accused of “assaulting” agents during immigration operations are U.S. citizens. Out of the 181 U.S. citizens accused on social media, none of them have been convicted at trial, and video evidence has frequently contradicted the government’s version of events. A majority of those U.S. citizens haven’t even had charges pressed, despite the department already charging and convicting them on social media. (Source)

There have also been significant failures in basic due process. A federal judge ordered the administration to bring back a man who was deported to Mexico in violation of a court-issued stay, noting that he was likely denied access to legal counsel and proper translation services. (Source)

In a separate case, ICE labeled a man “the worst of the worst” and held him for six weeks, despite ending up dropping his charges. The judge ordered his release. (Source)

In St. Paul, a contempt hearing was held because ICE failed to return the IDs, phones, and cash of 28 immigrants who were released into the cold without even warm clothing. (Source)

Another judge ordered ICE to return essential personal property, such as work authorizations and licenses, to six detainees or face further contempt charges. (Source)

Reports have also surfaced about deteriorating conditions inside ICE detention centers, where many migrants are held for over six months despite being willing to accept deportation, and where a string of suicides has drawn attention to a facility in Texas. (Source) (Source)

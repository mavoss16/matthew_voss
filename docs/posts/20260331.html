There is a pattern emerging that should concern everyone, regardless of your views on immigration: the administration is not simply pursuing aggressive enforcement — it is openly defying federal courts and doing so at a scale that judges themselves are calling historic. The rule of law depends on the executive branch following court orders. When it doesn’t, everything else falls apart.

Courts vs. The Administration

The relationship between the federal judiciary and the Trump administration has deteriorated sharply. Federal judges across multiple states are expressing frustration that goes well beyond ordinary friction over contested policy.

In Minnesota, Chief Judge Patrick Schiltz warned of criminal contempt charges after finding the government had violated more than 200 court orders in his district alone — a level of defiance he called historic. (Source) Nationally, there have been at least 35 instances since August where judges have demanded that the administration explain why it should not be punished for violating court orders. (Source)

In New Jersey, Judge Zahid Quraishi ruled that federal prosecutors have lost their credibility by “intentionally” ignoring judicial mandates — a finding serious enough that he may now require sworn testimony for future arrests. (Source) The Justice Department itself acknowledged violating more than 50 court orders in New Jersey, offering “sky-high caseloads” as the explanation for illegal deportations and missed deadlines. (Source) That is an explanation, not a justification. No private citizen gets to break the law and cite a busy schedule.

A tracking database of federal judges ruling on mass detention policy shows that an overwhelming majority — judges appointed by presidents of both parties — have rejected the current mandatory detention approach. (Source) This is not a partisan rebuke.

ICE Enforcement: Who Is Actually Being Targeted?

The administration has consistently claimed it is focused on dangerous criminals. The data tells a different story.

Nearly 75% of ICE detainees have no criminal conviction, according to ICE’s own data. (Source) Trump-appointed judges have issued rulings against “unimaginable cruelty” in cases involving individuals with no criminal records whatsoever. (Source)

Judge Tostrud found that ICE and CBP have adopted policies authorizing investigatory stops based solely on race or ethnicity — not on any individualized suspicion of wrongdoing. (Source) That is not immigration enforcement. That is racial profiling enshrined in policy.

A Wall Street Journal investigation found that the majority of people accused of “assaulting” agents during immigration operations are U.S. citizens. None of them have been convicted at trial, and video evidence has frequently contradicted the government’s version of events. (Source)

There have also been significant failures in basic due process. A federal judge ordered the administration to bring back a man who was deported to Mexico in violation of a court-issued stay, noting that he was likely denied access to legal counsel and proper translation services. (Source) In a separate case, ICE labeled a man “the worst of the worst” and held him for six weeks without charges — a judge threatened contempt and ordered his release. (Source) In St. Paul, a contempt hearing was held because ICE failed to return the IDs, phones, and cash of 28 immigrants who were released into the cold without even warm clothing. (Source) Judge Tunheim subsequently ordered ICE to return essential personal property — including work authorizations and licenses — to six detainees or face further contempt charges. (Source)

Reports have also surfaced about deteriorating conditions inside ICE detention centers, where many migrants are held for over six months despite being willing to accept deportation, and where a string of suicides has drawn attention to a facility in Texas. (Source) (Source)

The administration often says these individuals should have come here legally. That argument has nothing to do with whether the government gets to detain someone for six weeks without charges, deport someone in defiance of a court order, or seize personal property and simply not return it. Those are not immigration policy questions. Those are basic legal and human rights questions.

Pardons

The Cato Institute — not a left-leaning organization — has analyzed what it describes as a “blizzard” of second-term pardons, noting that over $1.5 billion in criminal debt has been forgiven for political allies and donors. (Source) For context, previous administrations wiped out far smaller sums through clemency. (Source) The traditional vetting process for pardons has been bypassed entirely.

This continues a trend I have written about before. The pardon power is being used not to correct injustices in the legal system, but to reward loyalty and erase consequences for those in the president’s orbit.

The Pentagon and Conflicts of Interest

ProPublica has investigated a “web of financial ties” involving Deputy Secretary of Defense Steve Feinberg and other officials who retain links to the industries they now regulate. (Source) This is exactly the kind of structural corruption that should draw bipartisan concern. When the people making decisions about defense contracts have personal financial stakes in the outcomes, the public interest is not being served.

A Note on AI and the Military

One story worth watching: there is an escalating conflict between the Department of Defense and Anthropic — the company that makes the Claude AI model — over usage restrictions that Anthropic has placed on its product in military contexts. (Source) Secretary Hegseth has threatened to designate Anthropic as a supply-chain risk over the dispute. (Source)

I am not taking a position here on what rules AI companies should or shouldn’t impose. But the idea that a company declining to remove its own safety restrictions from a product it built could be designated a national security threat is deeply troubling. It suggests the administration believes it can coerce private companies into removing guardrails by threatening them with government blacklisting. That is not how a free market is supposed to work.
